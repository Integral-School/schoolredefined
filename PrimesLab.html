Prime Numbers Lab

Hands-on games for the maths behind the Riemann Hypothesis story. Made for a child of about six and a grown-up, side by side.

What is a prime number? A prime is a whole number bigger than 1 that only 1 and itself divide exactly, such as 2, 3, 5, 7 and 11. Every other whole number can be made by multiplying primes together, so the primes are the building blocks of all numbers.

The prime hunt. Press 2, then 3, then 5, then 7. Each press crosses out every number you reach counting in jumps of that size, because those numbers can be split into equal groups and so are not prime. The yellow numbers left standing are the primes up to 100.

Goal: by crossing out everything that can be built by multiplying, you find the numbers that cannot be built from smaller ones. This method is more than two thousand years old.

Story so far

Prime numbers are the numbers you can only lay out in one long line, like 2, 3, 5, 7 and 11. They seem to pop up at random, and for a very long time nobody could explain the pattern behind them.

More than 150 years ago a mathematician called Bernhard Riemann made a famous guess. He found a machine of numbers whose secret helpers push the prime count up or down, and he guessed that every helper stands on one straight line. If he is right, the primes never stray far from a smooth path. People call this guess the Riemann Hypothesis, and nobody managed to prove it.

In November 2026 a mathematician called Levent Alpöge said he had found a proof, working with an AI helper called Claude. The proof is so long that no person can read all of it, so he gave it to Lean, a computer teacher that checks every step. Lean found no missing dominoes. Mathematicians around the world now want to understand the proof in words people can follow, and that work has only just begun.

The games below let you try each idea yourself, one step at a time.

A little history

People have studied primes for thousands of years

Around 300 BC the Greek mathematician Euclid proved that the primes never run out: however far you count, there is always another one. He also showed that every whole number is made from primes in only one way, like a recipe with just one list of ingredients.

About a hundred years later, Eratosthenes, a Greek scholar living in Alexandria in Egypt, invented the crossing-out method you use in the hundred square. It is still called the Sieve of Eratosthenes.

Much later, mathematicians such as Carl Friedrich Gauss made long tables of primes and noticed a pattern: primes get rarer at a steady, predictable rate. Gauss guessed a formula for how many primes there are below any number. Riemann then found a new way to study that question, and his ideas helped two mathematicians, Hadamard and de la Vallée Poussin, prove the formula at the end of the 1800s.

One question was left over. The formula gives the average, but how far can the real count of primes stray from it? Riemann's famous guess is about exactly that question, and the games below build up to it.

Big question

Is there a pattern in the primes?

Yes, although nobody has found a simple rule that tells you the next prime. The patterns come in three sizes.

Small patterns you can see. After 2, every prime is odd, because an even number can always be split into two equal rows. After 3 the pattern gets tighter: every prime sits one step away from a multiple of 6. For example, 5 and 7 sit either side of 6, 11 and 13 either side of 12, and 17 and 19 either side of 18. Write the numbers in rows of six and the primes fall into just two columns. Not every number in those columns is prime, though; 25 is 5 times 5.

A big pattern in how often primes appear. Gauss looked at long tables of primes and guessed that they thin out at a steady, predictable rate. Hadamard and de la Vallée Poussin proved his guess in 1896, and it is now called the Prime Number Theorem. So the average pattern is known exactly.

A hidden pattern in the wobbles. The real primes wobble around that average, arriving a little early here and a little late there. Riemann found that the wobbles are steered by his helper numbers. If all the helpers stand on one line, as he guessed, the wobbles stay as small and orderly as they possibly can. That is the hidden pattern, and it is what the Riemann Hypothesis says.

The next two games let you see the first two patterns, and the one after that shows the third as a picture.

Pattern in the scale

Each time the numbers grow ten times, the gaps grow by the same step

Single gaps between primes jump about, but their average follows a steady rule. Around any number, the average distance to the next prime is close to that number's natural logarithm, a quantity that grows by the same amount, about 2.3, every time the number gets ten times bigger.

Why it stayed a riddle

Knowing the average is easier than knowing the worst wobble

The 1896 theorem tells you the average: how many primes to expect below a number. It says little about how far the real count can stray from that average. Riemann's guess is about exactly this: if every zero lies on his line, the real count never strays further than it must, and the wobbles around the curve stay as small as they can be.

The difficulty is that there are infinitely many zeros. Computers have checked billions of them one by one and found every one on the line, but no list of checks reaches all of them. Mathematicians therefore tried to prove that at least a share of the zeros sits on the line. Earlier in 2026 an unreleased version of Claude raised that proven share to more than two in three, according to Anthropic, and the company said this did not settle the hypothesis.

Alpöge's claimed proof covers every zero. In his announcement note he describes an argument descended from a technique used over function fields and built from chained transformations between automorphic forms. A written version produced by machine runs past a quarter of a million pages; he called it unreadable and released the Lean code instead. A verification report by MIT mathematician Andrew Sutherland found that the checked result covers the classical hypothesis and a generalised form for one family of functions, Dirichlet L-functions.

Mathematicians now want a version that people can read and understand. The Clay Mathematics Institute, which offers a prize for the problem, requires publication followed by two years of scrutiny, so the riddle is closed for the computer and still open for human understanding.

What you just learned: Riemann's guess stayed open for more than 160 years because it is a claim about infinitely many zeros, and no amount of checking examples can prove it. Only a proof that covers every case can, and that is what Lean checked in November 2026.

Riemann's machine has secret helper numbers. Each helper nudges the prime count up or down. He guessed they all stand on one straight line. Press the button to push one helper off the line and watch the count wobble more.

Activity 6

Dominoes

Goal: understand what a proof is, and what the computer checked.

A proof works like a row of dominoes. Each step knocks over the next, so if the first falls, they all fall. A computer teacher called Lean checks that no domino is missing.

What you just learned: a proof is a chain of steps in which each step makes the next one certain, and one missing step breaks the whole chain. Lean checks that no step is missing. Mathematicians still want to read the proof, to understand why the chain was built that way.

Chaos

Goal: see what scientists mean by chaos before reading what the proof means for it.

Two marbles start almost in the same spot, only a hair apart. In a chaotic machine their paths soon look nothing alike. Scientists noticed that Riemann's helpers keep their distance the way energy levels do in chaotic systems.

The paths come from a simple chaotic rule, drawn as an illustration.

What you just learned: in a chaotic system a tiny difference at the start grows into a huge difference later. The next box explains why chaos comes up in the story of the primes.

Big question

What it means for chaos

Scientists who study chaos noticed something strange about Riemann's helpers. The helpers keep their distance from each other in the same pattern as the energy levels of tiny particles bouncing around inside chaotic machines. Some physicists began to wonder whether a hidden chaotic machine makes the primes, with each prime acting like a path the machine keeps repeating.

Much of that work started with an "if". It said: if Riemann's guess is true, then this follows. A proof that holds up turns those "if" results into things we know for sure.

The proof does not find the hidden machine. Alpöge's argument uses tools from pure mathematics with no physics machine inside it, so the link between primes and chaos is still a puzzle waiting for an explanation.

The primes still teach a lesson. They look random from the outside, yet every single one is fixed by a rule, so what looked like chaos was an intricate order we had not yet learned to see.
